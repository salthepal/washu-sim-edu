## Start with the performance, not the product

Simulation technology is useful when it lets learners practice a meaningful
clinical action, see the consequence of a decision, or make their performance
available for feedback. The product itself is not the learning activity. A
manikin, task trainer, standardized participant, virtual environment, monitor,
or game engine provides capabilities; the educator still has to design the
experience.

Use this sequence before selecting a modality:

1. Name the educational gap.
2. Write the learner behavior that would demonstrate the objective.
3. Identify what the environment must sense, display, or allow.
4. Choose the lowest-complexity approach that can do those things credibly.
5. Decide how learners will receive feedback and how performance will be evaluated.

For a tourniquet objective, the decisive function may be controllable bleeding
and realistic limb compression. A full-body manikin's heart sounds, speech, and
physiologic model may add cost and operator workload without helping the
objective. For a resuscitation leadership objective, the important functions
may instead be an evolving monitor, team roles, workload, shared cues, and a
reliable way to observe communication.

This is curricular **fit**: the objective, activity, technology, and assessment
all point toward the same learner performance. Technology selected mainly for
novelty can add cognitive load and compete with the curriculum.

## Match the modality to the objective

No single modality is best for simulation. Choose what makes the target
behavior possible and observable.

| Educational need | Useful starting modality | Function to verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Practice a discrete procedure | Task trainer or mixed physical-virtual trainer | Anatomical landmarks, tissue response, tool compatibility, and performance feedback |
| Manage an evolving unstable patient | Manikin plus monitor or screen-based patient | Coherent responses, observable decision points, and reliable state changes |
| Practice history, empathy, conflict, or disclosure | Standardized participant, conversational virtual patient, or hybrid format | Natural interaction, consistent role portrayal, and safe handling of emotional content |
| Rehearse team leadership and coordination | In-situ, manikin-based, virtual multiplayer, or hybrid simulation | Roles, communication channels, workload, shared cues, and team-level observation |
| Practice rare events across distance or asynchronously | Standalone virtual simulation or branching case | Clear instructions, access, compatibility, saved progress, and feedback without a live instructor |
| Orient learners to a location or workflow | In-situ rehearsal, virtual environment, walkthrough, or tutorial | Accurate spatial and process cues without irrelevant detail |

Modalities can be combined when they form one learning sequence. Learners might
triage a mass-casualty event asynchronously, then manage the same patients in a
team-based simulation and debrief the transition between settings.

## Describe the fidelity that matters

Terms such as *high fidelity* and *low fidelity* are imprecise. Fidelity depends
on the learner, the objective, and the function being represented. Separate the
complexity of the technology from the credibility of the experience.

| Term | Practical meaning | Design implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technology level | The complexity and capability of the tool or platform | Describe the functions you need instead of using high or low as a product label |
| Functional realism | How credibly a specific function behaves, such as chest recoil, bleeding control, airway anatomy, or monitor response | Test the exact function tied to the objective; a feature list does not establish realism |
| Experiential fidelity | How convincingly the whole scenario represents the relevant practice for the learner | Narrative, roles, cues, timing, and consequences may matter more than hardware |
| Physical or environmental fidelity | How closely the body, equipment, or setting resembles clinical practice | Include the realism needed for learner action and transfer |
| Psychological or emotional fidelity | Whether the experience creates the relevant attention, uncertainty, responsibility, or social pressure | Build challenge deliberately without using surprise or threat as a substitute for good design |

A monitor-only case with coherent decisions and consequences can feel more
credible than an expensive manikin in an incoherent scenario. State what needs
to be realistic and why.

## Decide how the patient will respond

The way a manikin or virtual patient changes during a case affects consistency,
operator workload, and the risk of teaching an implausible physiologic
relationship. Three approaches often overlap.

| Operating approach | How it works | Strength | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Manual input, or running on the fly | The operator changes vital signs, rhythms, sounds, and other parameters as the case unfolds | Flexible when learners take an unanticipated path | Responses depend on operator attention and knowledge; related variables may become inconsistent |
| Physiologic modeling | A mathematical model changes linked variables after an intervention or altered parameter | Can produce coordinated responses and reduce manual workload | The model may behave unexpectedly or make a desired teaching state difficult to produce |
| State-based modeling or scripting | The author defines patient states and transitions triggered by learner actions, time, or events | Supports reproducibility and lets several parameters change together | Quality depends on clinical logic, programming, testing, and handling alternate learner actions |

The most automated approach is not automatically the safest or most useful. If
using manual input, script expected physiologic relationships and assign an
operator who can focus on the patient. If using a physiologic model, test the
full case rather than assuming it will produce the intended course. If using
states, define expected and off-path transitions and avoid runtime overrides
that break the script's logic.

### Prevent negative teaching

Technology can teach the wrong lesson when a function looks plausible but
behaves incorrectly. Examples include chest compressions with unrealistic
resistance, a persistent blood pressure after prolonged apnea, or a treatment
that produces an inappropriate monitor response.

Before learners use the case:

1. Test every function essential to an objective.
2. Walk through the expected learner path and at least one alternate path.
3. Check that linked findings change together in a clinically coherent way.
4. Confirm what the operator may change without breaking the model or script.
5. Decide which limitations learners need to hear during the prebrief.

## Use virtual and game-based tools intentionally

A virtual setting is only a digital place where activity can occur. A designed
learning experience adds a role, goal, relevant decisions, consequences,
feedback, and a connection to the curriculum.

- **Virtual simulation** occurs primarily in a digital environment, such as a
  screen-based encounter, branching case, or immersive world.
- **Game-based learning** organizes learning through rules, goals, challenge,
  feedback, and meaningful consequences.
- **Gamification** adds selected game elements, such as progress indicators or
  levels, to a non-game activity. Points and badges alone do not make a
  simulation.
- **Minigames or tutorials** can teach navigation, controls, or equipment use
  before the clinical task.
- **Hybrid simulation** combines modalities into a connected experience, such
  as virtual triage followed by manikin-based resuscitation.

The central question remains what learners will do. A visually accurate
emergency department without a purposeful task is a virtual tour. Add a
clinical role, meaningful decisions, consequences, and feedback, and it becomes
a designed experience.

Orientation is part of the design. Give learners a low-stakes way to learn
controls, navigation, communication tools, and help systems before those
mechanics affect performance. When the objective is clinical reasoning, do not
accidentally assess familiarity with an interface.

## Brief the people running the case

Standardized patients, confederates, embedded participants, and technology
operators need clear roles, emotional tone, boundaries, and cueing plans. They
should know:

- what information to offer freely and what to reveal only if asked;
- when to escalate, hold, or step out of the interaction;
- which learner actions trigger a response or patient-state change;
- how to communicate behind the scenes with the facilitator; and
- which behaviors would derail the objective or psychological safety.

Briefing is especially important when a participant creates pressure. In the
pediatric drowning case, a panicked parent can create a realistic competing
demand, but the role should still allow learners to acknowledge the parent,
delegate communication, and continue the child's resuscitation. The pressure
should reveal team behavior without becoming harassment or overpowering the
case.

## Build the operating plan

Translate the scenario progression into a shared operator map. This connects
learner actions, participant cues, technology changes, and backups.

| Case event | Expected learner action | Planned response | Operator or trigger | Backup cue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Patient becomes apneic | Recognize respiratory failure and ventilate | Respiratory rate falls; oxygen saturation and heart rate trend appropriately | Scripted state or manikin operator | Facilitator announces absent respirations and trends the monitor manually |
| Learner gives treatment | Reassess the patient | Relevant findings improve, worsen, or remain unchanged | Sensor, operator input, or virtual rule | Facilitator provides a timed verbal or printed update |
| Team requests available data | Seek and interpret additional information | Result appears through the expected clinical channel | Confederate or virtual interface | Facilitator supplies the result directly |
| Technology or prop fails | Continue toward the same clinical decision | Case proceeds with reduced realism | Lead facilitator | Pause briefly, narrate the missing finding, and resume |

Assign one person to lead the scenario and make clear who controls the patient,
monitor, audio, results, embedded participants, and time. If one operator holds
several roles, simplify the number of simultaneous cues and transitions.

## Dry run in the real environment

A dry run checks whether the written case works in the room where it will be
used. Test the actual equipment, network, accounts, slides, monitors,
microphones, task trainers, medications, props, timing, and facilitator
handoffs.

The dry run should answer four questions:

1. Can learners perceive every cue needed to meet the objective?
2. Do patient findings and technology responses remain clinically coherent?
3. Can the team operate the case without missing competing tasks?
4. Does the backup preserve the same decision point and debrief hook?

Walk through the expected path and at least one plausible alternate path. Dry
runs often reveal that a case is too crowded. If the facilitator needs too many
simultaneous cues, changes, or technology moves, simplify before learners
arrive.

## Adapt when the case goes off plan

Cases rarely run exactly as written. Learners may get stuck, chase a trivial
detail, avoid touching the patient, or move faster than expected. A participant
may miss a cue. Technology may fail.

When something breaks, protect the objective first. Depending on the problem,
the facilitator can:

- allow time for learners to recognize and recover;
- provide a planned cue through the patient, nurse, parent, monitor, or result;
- simplify or narrate a finding that the technology cannot provide;
- pause and restart if the fiction has become confusing; or
- stop the scenario when continuing would undermine safety or learning.

In the debrief, acknowledge technology and facilitation problems directly so
learners are not blamed for cues they could not perceive.

## Evaluate the case as a complete system

Available technology data are not automatically useful evidence. Evaluate
whether the designed experience worked at three levels:

1. **Learner performance:** Did learners demonstrate the target behavior,
   improve with repetition, or transfer the skill to a later setting?
2. **Educational implementation:** Could learners understand and participate
   in the activity? Did orientation, facilitation, feedback, and debriefing work?
3. **Technology performance:** Did the tools behave reliably and credibly? How
   much staff time, troubleshooting, downtime, and support did they require?

Event logs may show decision paths, time in a state, resource use, or repeated
attempts. Interpret those measures against the objective. A shorter completion
time could reflect mastery, guessing, or familiarity with the interface. Pair
system data with observation and learner reasoning when the distinction
matters.

After each run, decide what to keep, revise, or remove. Continued use should
depend on learning value, reliability, access, and resource demand—not on the
complexity or purchase price of the technology.

## When selecting technology for the program

For a larger purchase or program-level technology decision, look beyond the
demonstration and acquisition price. Confirm educational fit, have intended
users test the exact functions in realistic conditions, and account for
staffing, training, accessories, consumables, software, maintenance, storage,
support, accessibility, data handling, and eventual replacement.

A capable product can still fail if the program cannot staff, maintain,
integrate, or schedule it. Document ownership, service terms, required
infrastructure, renewal dates, and a downtime plan before committing.

## Working product

By the end of this module, add an operating page to your case that includes:

1. The objective and observable learner action.
2. The selected modality and the exact functions it must perform.
3. The fidelity that matters and what can be simplified.
4. Participant and operator roles.
5. Expected patient states, triggers, and alternate paths.
6. The dry-run checklist.
7. A backup that preserves the central decision point.
8. One learner, implementation, and technology measure to review after the run.

This turns a written scenario into a shared plan that another simulation team
can run, troubleshoot, and improve.
