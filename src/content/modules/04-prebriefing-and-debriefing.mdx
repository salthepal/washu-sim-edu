## Debriefing starts before the case

The debrief is not a cleanup conversation after the "real" simulation. It is
where much of the learning is made explicit. Palaganas, Littlewood, and Szyld
describe debriefing as a core part of simulation education because it helps
participants reconsider how they think and practice, not just whether they
completed a checklist.

That means debriefing begins during case design. The objective determines what
moments must occur in the scenario, what the facilitator should watch for, how
much time the debrief needs, and what kind of method will fit. A case with no
debrief plan often drifts toward a mini-lecture, a list of missed facts, or a
discussion of whatever felt most dramatic.

Before learners enter the room, the facilitator should know:

1. Which objective is the debrief built around?
2. Which scenario moment will likely reveal the learner's reasoning?
3. What result or performance gap might be visible?
4. What learner frames could explain the actions?
5. What take-home behavior or mental model should learners leave with?

For most simulation sessions, protect real time for the debrief. A short case
with a rushed debrief is usually a poor trade. The debrief may need as much time
as the scenario or more, especially when the objective involves clinical
reasoning, teamwork, communication, or systems thinking.

## Prebriefing sets the learning contract

The prebrief creates the conditions for the scenario and the debrief that
follows. Learners are vulnerable in simulation because they are performing under
observation in an artificial environment. A strong prebrief reduces avoidable
threat without removing challenge.

A practical prebrief should address:

- **Purpose:** What the session is for and how it connects to the curriculum.
- **Basic assumption:** Participants are capable, committed, and trying to
  improve.
- **Fiction contract:** The simulation will not be perfectly real; learners and
  facilitators agree to work with its limitations in good faith.
- **Confidentiality:** What stays in the room and what, if anything, is
  documented or assessed.
- **Environment:** Room layout, monitor behavior, manikin or standardized
  patient limitations, available equipment, medications, and how to call for
  help.
- **Expected behavior:** Whether learners should act as they would clinically,
  verbalize actions that cannot be performed, touch the patient, delegate tasks,
  or ask for unavailable data.
- **Debrief process:** Learners should know that the debrief will include their
  reactions, reconstruction of key events, analysis of decisions, and summary of
  changes for future practice.
- **Participation norms:** Respectful challenge is expected. The group will
  critique behaviors, decisions, and systems, not personal worth.

The prebrief is also where the facilitator prevents common friction points. If
the case requires learners to examine the manikin, say so. If medications are
simulated, clarify how orders and administration should occur. If the scenario
will include a distressed family member or an embedded participant, explain that
the role exists to support the objective, not to trick or humiliate anyone.

## Psychological safety is not comfort

Psychological safety means learners can take interpersonal risks: speak up,
make uncertainty visible, admit confusion, ask questions, and examine mistakes
without being shamed. It does not mean the facilitator avoids difficult topics.

The Rudolph, Simon, Dufresne, and Raemer paper is useful because it challenges
the idea that debriefers can be truly "nonjudgmental." Facilitators notice
things, hold clinical standards, and make judgments about performance. If those
judgments are hidden behind vague questions, learners may feel confused,
manipulated, or left to guess what the facilitator really thinks.

The goal is not to have no judgment. The goal is good judgment: clear enough to
name the concern, humble enough to test the facilitator's interpretation, and
curious enough to understand why the learner's action made sense in the moment.

## Use a simple debriefing progression

Different debriefing methods use different names, but most debriefs move
through three broad phases: reactions, analysis, and summary. PEARLS and GAS
both fit this general arc, and the same progression can guide a novice
facilitator.

| Phase | Purpose | Facilitator moves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reactions | Clear the air, acknowledge emotion, and surface immediate concerns. | Ask how the case felt, listen actively, clarify major technical distractions, and note topics worth returning to. |
| Analysis | Explore the experience in relation to objectives and clinical practice. | Reconstruct key moments, compare expected and actual performance, explore learner frames, teach briefly when needed, and connect the case to future work. |
| Summary | Consolidate learning and translate it into action. | Ask learners for takeaways, name unresolved but important points, and define one or two changes they will carry forward. |

Do not skip the reactions phase just because it feels less efficient. Learners
may be self-critical, frustrated by technology, worried about peer judgment, or
stuck on a clinical fact. If those concerns are ignored, they often leak into
the analysis phase and prevent deeper reflection.

The analysis phase is where the facilitator spends most of the intellectual
effort. Choose a small number of high-yield moments. A debrief that tries to
cover every missed step usually becomes shallow. A debrief that explores one or
two important moments well can change future practice.

The summary should not be an instructor monologue. Whenever possible, ask
learners to state what they are taking away. The facilitator can then refine,
correct, and connect those takeaways to the objective.

## Choose the method that fits the moment

No single debriefing method is best for every simulation. The right choice
depends on the objective, available time, learner experience, emotional charge,
and facilitator skill.

| Method | Best fit | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plus-delta | Quick review of what worked and what should change, especially for technical skills or brief sessions. | Vague entries such as "good teamwork" or "better communication." Push for specific behaviors. |
| GAS | Standardized programs that need a clear gather, analyze, summarize structure. | Over-structuring can feel procedural if the facilitator does not listen for learner priorities. |
| 4Es | Sessions where events, emotions, empathy, and explanation are central, especially with complex interpersonal dynamics. | The facilitator still needs to connect discussion back to the objective and future action. |
| PEARLS | Flexible debriefs where the facilitator may blend learner self-assessment, focused facilitation, directive feedback, and teaching. | Flexibility requires judgment; avoid using the framework as permission to wander. |
| Advocacy-inquiry / good judgment | Clinical reasoning, teamwork, prioritization, communication, and moments where learner frames matter. | It requires real curiosity. If the question is a disguised accusation, learners will hear it. |

For this curriculum, advocacy-inquiry is especially useful because the exemplar
cases are designed around decision points. The point is not just whether the
learner gave epinephrine or assigned a team leader. The point is what they saw,
assumed, prioritized, feared, or misunderstood at the time.

## Debrief actions, frames, and results

The good-judgment model links three things:

- **Results:** What happened in the scenario, such as delayed epinephrine,
  persistent hypotension, role confusion, or smooth escalation.
- **Actions:** What learners did or did not do.
- **Frames:** The knowledge, assumptions, emotions, priorities, and mental
  models that made those actions seem reasonable in the moment.

Actions and results are visible. Frames are not. The debriefer's job is to use
visible performance as an entry point into learner reasoning. This matters
because the same action can come from different frames. A resident may delay
epinephrine because they do not know the indication, because they are anchored
on asthma, because they fear medication harm, because the parent is pushing a
different explanation, or because no one owns the medication role. Each frame
needs a different teaching response.

## Write advocacy-inquiry in three parts

Advocacy-inquiry pairs the facilitator's view with genuine curiosity about the
learner's view.

Use this template:

1. **Observation:** "I noticed..."
2. **Point of view:** "I was concerned because..." or "I thought that helped because..."
3. **Inquiry:** "What were you seeing or thinking at that moment?"

Examples:

> I noticed epinephrine was not given until after the second set of vital signs.
> I was concerned because the child had respiratory symptoms plus hypotension,
> and delayed epinephrine can worsen anaphylaxis. What were you considering at
> that moment?

> I saw you step back, name yourself as team leader, and assign airway,
> medications, and documentation in the first minute. I thought that gave the
> team a clear structure early. What prompted you to do that?

> I noticed the group continued discussing nitrates after the inferior STEMI
> tracing and hypotension were recognized. I was worried about right-ventricular
> involvement and worsening preload dependence. How were you interpreting the
> ECG and blood pressure at that point?

Advocacy-inquiry is not a script for being polite. It is a disciplined way to
make errors and successes discussable. The facilitator states their concern or
appreciation clearly, then tests it against the learner's reasoning.

## Debrief the pediatric anaphylaxis case

The pediatric anaphylaxis exemplar has a clean debrief hook: the caregiver asks
for a breathing treatment "like last time," while the child has features of
anaphylaxis. If learners delay epinephrine, avoid asking, "Why didn't you give
epi?" That question may sound like a cross-examination and usually points only
to the action.

Use the frame:

> I heard the parent ask for a breathing treatment, and I saw the team move
> toward albuterol before epinephrine. I was concerned because the hypotension
> and airway symptoms made anaphylaxis the time-critical diagnosis. What was
> most salient to you when the parent made that request?

Possible frames include anchoring on prior wheezing, reluctance to give
epinephrine to a child, uncertainty about dose or route, or social pressure from
the caregiver. Once the frame is visible, the debrief can rehearse the future
move: name anaphylaxis out loud, assign medication dosing, give IM epinephrine,
and explain to the caregiver why the treatment differs from prior asthma care.

## Debrief the STEMI cardiogenic shock case

The STEMI case can support a senior-level debrief. The key issue may be less
about recognizing STEMI and more about prioritizing shock physiology,
contraindications, leadership, and escalation.

An advocacy-inquiry prompt might be:

> I noticed nitrates were discussed while the patient was hypotensive with an
> inferior STEMI pattern. I was concerned that this could worsen right-sided
> preload-dependent shock. What was your working model of the hypotension at
> that moment?

If the frame is "all chest pain gets nitro," the teaching response is different
from "we saw shock but did not know how to stabilize before cath" or "no one
owned the medication decision." The debrief should make the frame visible, then
connect it to a future action plan.

## When the scenario does not go as planned

A "failed" simulation can still produce useful learning, but the facilitator has
to protect the objective and the learners.

If learners perform poorly, proceed with respect and directness. Participants
often know when things went badly. Critique the behavior, decision, or system,
not the person. Integrity still requires naming unsafe or incorrect performance;
otherwise the debrief may accidentally endorse it.

If technology fails, decide whether to pause, narrate missing data, restart,
abort, or redirect the scenario. Then be transparent in the debrief. Do not let
learners spend the whole conversation litigating the manikin if the educational
objective can still be salvaged.

If a learner resists the scenario or debrief, first connect back to the clinical
relevance: "When you have seen this issue with a real patient, how have you
handled it?" If behavior disrupts the group, name the participation norm and
redirect. In rare cases, a private conversation after the session is more
appropriate than continuing to manage the behavior in front of peers.

## Improve the debriefer

Debriefing expertise develops the same way clinical expertise does: deliberate
practice, observation, coaching, feedback, and repeated attempts. Basic
structures can be learned quickly, but expert facilitation takes time.

After running a session, debrief the debriefer:

1. Did the prebrief establish the learning contract clearly?
2. Did the debrief stay aligned with the objective?
3. Did the facilitator make enough space for reactions?
4. Were the analysis topics prioritized, or did the conversation sprawl?
5. Did questions uncover learner frames or only review actions?
6. Were judgments stated clearly and respectfully?
7. Did learners leave with a concrete future action?

Quality tools such as DASH and OSAD exist for more formal debriefing assessment,
but the essential habit is simpler: watch debriefs, ask for feedback, practice
specific moves, and make debriefing quality part of the simulation program's
routine improvement work.

## Working product

By the end of this module, you should have a prebrief checklist, a debrief map
linked to your case objective, and at least one advocacy-inquiry prompt for a
high-yield moment. Those products make the simulation teachable: learners know
how to engage, facilitators know what to watch for, and the debrief has a clear
path from experience to future practice.
