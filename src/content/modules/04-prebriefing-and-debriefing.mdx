## Psychological safety and the fiction contract

Simulation asks learners to perform in an artificial environment while being
observed. The prebrief should lower unnecessary threat by naming the fiction
contract, confidentiality expectations, available equipment, facilitator roles,
and the basic assumption that everyone is capable, committed, and trying to
improve.

Psychological safety does not mean avoiding challenge. It means creating enough
trust that challenge can lead to learning.

## What belongs in the prebrief

A practical prebrief orients learners to the room, manikin or standardized
patient, monitors, medications, time frame, call-for-help rules, and any
simulation-specific limitations. It should also clarify whether learners should
act as they would clinically or verbalize actions that cannot be performed.

This is where the facilitator prevents common friction points, including the
"no touch" learner who narrates every action instead of examining or treating
the patient.

## Structured debriefing with PEARLS

Most of the learning in simulation happens in the debrief, not the scenario.
This module is where residents move from *running* a case to *teaching* with one.

PEARLS gives facilitators a flexible structure: reactions, description,
analysis, and summary. The structure keeps the conversation focused while still
allowing the facilitator to choose the right strategy for the moment.

## Advocacy-inquiry and learner frames

Pair an observation and your point of view (advocacy) with genuine curiosity
about the learner's reasoning (inquiry): *"I saw epinephrine was held until the
second set of vitals — I was concerned that delayed treatment. I'm curious what
you were seeing at that moment."*

Advocacy-inquiry is useful because it treats performance as the visible result
of an underlying frame. The debrief is not just about what happened; it is about
understanding how the learner interpreted the situation at the time.

## Adjusting the debrief to the objective

Not every interesting clinical issue deserves debrief time. Return to the
objective, the key decisions, and the moments that reveal learner reasoning. If
the group is junior, the debrief may focus on recognition and first actions. If
the group is senior, the same case may focus on leadership, prioritization, and
systems factors.
