## Why objectives come first

A good simulation case starts with a narrow answer to a practical question:
what should the learner be able to do differently after this experience? The
objective should name an observable decision, behavior, communication move, or
team process rather than a broad topic area.

Objectives also protect the case from becoming too busy. If a lab value,
confederate line, technology choice, or complication does not serve the
objective, it probably belongs in another case.

## Matching objectives to PGY level

Learner level should shape the case before the clinical content does. A PGY1
case may focus on recognizing a time-sensitive diagnosis and taking the first
correct action. A senior resident case can ask for parallel processing,
leadership, prioritization, systems awareness, or management of uncertainty.

The pediatric anaphylaxis exemplar is intentionally appropriate for junior
residents because the diagnosis is not the trick. The learning target is early
epinephrine under time pressure.

## Choosing the right amount of complexity

Complexity is useful only when it creates the right decision pressure. Add
noise when it reveals a learner's frame, exposes a common error, or gives the
debrief a teachable moment. Remove noise when it distracts from the intended
objective.

## Defining what success looks like

Before writing the scenario, define the behaviors that would count as success.
For example: gives IM epinephrine within the first few minutes, calls for help
before airway collapse, assigns roles clearly, or names right-ventricular
involvement before giving nitrates. Those success criteria become the backbone
for the scenario, facilitator cues, assessment notes, and debrief.
