## Start with the research question

Simulation research should begin with a focused question, not with a favorite
scenario or tool. A strong question identifies the learner group, intervention or
phenomenon, comparison if relevant, outcomes of interest, and timeframe.

The literature review should do more than prove that the topic is interesting.
It should show what is already known, what remains unclear, and which theory or
conceptual framework can guide the project.

## Classify the purpose of the study

One useful framework separates education research into three purposes:

1. **Description:** What is happening? These studies describe a curriculum,
   process, implementation, learner experience, or observed pattern.
2. **Justification:** Did it work? These studies test whether an intervention
   improves an outcome compared with a baseline or comparison condition.
3. **Clarification:** How or why did it work? These studies use theory and
   conceptual frameworks to explain mechanisms, context, and meaning.

Many novice projects stop at description or try to jump straight to proving that
a simulation "works." A stronger project asks what decision the study can
actually support and chooses the design accordingly.

## Align methods and outcomes

Quantitative methods are useful when the question involves measurable variables,
relationships, or intervention effects. Qualitative methods are useful when the
goal is to understand experience, meaning, behavior, or process. Mixed methods
can be useful when the project needs both outcome data and explanation.

Simulation studies also need careful attention to reliability and validity. If
the study uses performance scores, decide who will rate the performance, how
raters will be trained, which tool will be used, and what validity evidence
supports interpreting the results.

## Build the protocol before collecting data

A practical research protocol should include the problem statement, purpose,
research questions or hypotheses, literature review, methods, measures, analysis
plan, timeline, and required approvals. For resident or faculty simulation
projects, early input from a statistician, qualitative methodologist, librarian,
or experienced education researcher can prevent avoidable design problems.
